# World Models in AI: Predictive Representations, Generative Planning, and Evaluation

## TL;DR
- A useful operational definition is a model that predicts task-relevant future states or observations under candidate actions; it can inform planning, but is not inherently a policy or controller. [1][2]
- Current systems span compressed/latent dynamics, action-conditioned video generation, flow-guided planning, and latent-action models; these design choices trade off visual detail, action interface, and computational tractability. [3][4]
- Evaluation is shifting from visual quality toward action fidelity, physical plausibility, long-horizon stability, and execution; benchmark results cited here are not directly comparable across protocols. [5][6][7][8]
- The evidence base retrieved for this survey is concentrated in 2025–2026 and includes several HF-generated summaries; these are discovery evidence, not substitutes for paper verification. Exact dates are unavailable for some web records. [9][10][8][11]

## Background
“World model” has no single settled boundary. One framing emphasizes internal representations that capture the present world; another centers predicting future states so agents can simulate outcomes and make decisions. [12] A 2026 survey describes world models as learned internal simulators that support prediction, planning, and reasoning in learned representations. [1] For robotics, an especially practical definition is conditional: estimate how future observations or states evolve under candidate actions. [2]

This definition separates the model from what uses it. A predictive model can supply imagined trajectories to model-predictive control, evaluation, or synthetic data, but prediction alone does not choose or execute an action. [2] Architectures may predict in compact latent space or directly in observation/video space; these are representational choices rather than competing definitions of world modeling. [2][12] The distinction matters because a plausible-looking future is not, by itself, evidence that a system represents actionable dynamics or supports reliable control. [2][7]

## Predictive representations and action interfaces
A key design choice is what the model predicts and how action enters. Latent representations aim to preserve decision-relevant information while filtering redundant detail, whereas video-space models expose richer visual predictions. [2][12] Action conditioning gives the prediction a control interface by making candidate actions part of the model’s inputs. [2] Yet a conditional interface alone does not demonstrate reliable response to interventions or real-world controllability. [2][7] 

Other methods build explicit action abstractions. AdaWorld’s abstract describes self-supervised extraction of latent actions from video and autoregressive world modeling conditioned on those actions. [4] The summary reports improvements in simulation quality and visual planning across environments, but supplies no numerical outcome in the retrieved passage. [4] These approaches illustrate a recurring trade-off: compact action/dynamics representations can make planning more tractable, while video-based prediction can retain visual context; neither alone guarantees the correct response to interventions. [2][4][6]

## Generative models as planners
Generative video models are increasingly used inside planning pipelines, rather than treated only as video synthesis. FLIP combines multimodal flow generation for action proposals, flow-conditioned video generation as a dynamics module, and a learned value component. [3] The paper abstract reports experiments across diverse benchmarks and claims improved success rates and long-horizon plan quality; the retrieved evidence does not provide enough detail to compare effect sizes across tasks. [3]

The retrieved evidence also includes latent-action conditioning: AdaWorld’s abstract describes self-supervised extraction of latent actions from video and autoregressive world modeling conditioned on them. [4] FLIP combines multimodal flow generation for action proposals, flow-conditioned video generation as a dynamics module, and a learned value component. [3] Its abstract reports experiments across diverse benchmarks and claims improved success rates and long-horizon plan quality, but the retrieved evidence does not provide enough detail to compare effect sizes across tasks. [3] Together these examples show that learned dynamics, action proposals, and value or evaluation signals can be composed; reliability still depends on each interface. [2][3][4]

## From plausible video to physical and executable behavior
Evaluation studies challenge the use of visual fidelity as a proxy for physical understanding. WorldModelBench evaluates video models for instruction following and physics adherence, with 67K human labels across 14 models according to the retrieved paper text; it reports common difficulty in autonomous driving, human activities, and robotics. [6] A separate procedural benchmark reports that frontier models reach at most 57% on its world-model task and 38% on its procedural-planning task, while humans solve both perfectly. [5] These task-specific gaps indicate that recognizable or fluent video does not automatically encode causal state changes or ordered action sequences. [5][6]

Embodied benchmarks make the distinction more concrete by testing whether generated behaviors can be translated into actions and executed. RoboWM-Bench’s retrieved text states that “visual realism does not imply physical plausibility” and reports problems with spatial reasoning, contact stability, and execution as task complexity rises. [7] HF’s AI-generated summary for World-in-World describes a closed-loop evaluation platform and argues that open-loop protocols focused on visual quality can miss embodied utility. [8] Another HF summary says X-WAM unifies robotic action execution with 4D world synthesis and predicts multi-view RGB-D video; this is a reported system description, not independently verified performance evidence. [10] Collectively, the work motivates evaluating action-following and execution alongside image/video metrics. [7][10][8]

## Long-horizon reliability, generalization, and limits
Short rollouts can hide drift and compounding errors. A 2026 survey flags compounding prediction errors, sim-to-real transfer, and fragmented evaluation as continuing challenges. [1] HF’s AI-generated GigaWorld-1 summary emphasizes long-horizon rollout consistency and robot-specific controllability. [9] This summary supports a concern, but does not establish a universal ranking or quantify real-world transfer; the retrieved material offers limited independently verified evidence on out-of-distribution generalization.

The evidence also has methodological limits. Several benchmark records were retrieved as website search text rather than independently inspected full papers; HF-daily and HF-search material is explicitly summary-only. [9][10][8][11] Publication dates are blank where the retrieval output did not supply them, so those works cannot be precisely placed within the two-year window from these records alone. [3][5][6][7] Claims and percentages should therefore be treated as reported by the named sources, not independently reproduced. Most importantly, evaluation dimensions can confound each other: poor action fidelity may look like memory failure, and visual similarity can coexist with physically impossible interaction. [5][9][7]

## Trends and open problems
The recent evidence points toward three complementary directions: action-conditioned prediction, systems that couple prediction to proposals/value/feedback, and closed-loop evaluation grounded in execution. [2][3][4][10][8] Benchmark authors increasingly ask whether a model follows interventions, preserves physical constraints, and remains stable through extended interaction, rather than whether individual frames look convincing. [6][9][7][8][11] This is a shift in evaluation emphasis, not yet proof that current systems achieve robust general-purpose world models.

Open problems include maintaining object identity and geometry over long rollouts, separating genuine causal dynamics from dataset correlations, measuring out-of-distribution and sim-to-real generalization, and building reproducible tests that connect prediction quality to downstream task success. [1][5][9][7] Evidence remains uneven: the retrieved set has stronger support for benchmark-specific limitations than for broad claims of real-world generalization, and HF summaries should be verified against the underlying papers before detailed technical or quantitative use. [9][10][8][11] As of the user-specified date, this survey can establish promising architectural patterns and evaluation concerns, but not a single best architecture or a settled empirical standard for “world model” capability. [1][12][6]
