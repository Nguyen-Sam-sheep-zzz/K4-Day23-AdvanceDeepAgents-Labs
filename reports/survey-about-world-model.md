# World Models for Learning, Simulation, and Control: An Evidence-Grounded Survey

## TL;DR
- A useful operational definition is a learned model that predicts environment state or observations under actions, enabling forecasting, planning, or interaction; the seminal *World Models* paper formalized this as compressed visual representations, recurrent latent dynamics, and a controller trained with imagined experience. [1]
- The research line extends from latent imagination for visual control and physical robot learning toward action-conditioned video simulators, but these are different operating regimes and their results are not directly comparable. [2][3][4][5]
- Evidence increasingly argues against using visual plausibility as a stand-in for usefulness: closed-loop task success, action adherence, temporal consistency, physics, and transfer must be tested separately. [6][7][8][9]
- The evidence base is uneven: much of the recent evidence is a paper's own abstract/full text or HF's AI-generated paper-page summary, and benchmark results do not establish general capability across environments. [7][8][10][11][12]

## Background
The foundational *World Models* paper (2018) decomposes a visual control problem into a vision model that compresses observations, a recurrent dynamics model that predicts latent codes in light of actions, and a controller that acts on the resulting state representation. Its authors describe training the controller within the model's generated “dream” and transferring it to the environment; this is a primary-paper account of a method, not evidence that imagined dynamics are universally faithful. [1] The central conceptual move is to make prediction useful for action, rather than treating image generation alone as the end goal.

*Dream to Control* (Dreamer) develops latent imagination into an optimization procedure: its abstract describes solving long-horizon visual tasks using imagined trajectories and propagating analytic value gradients through them. [2] A distinct deployment step appears in *DayDreamer*, whose abstract reports online world-model learning on four physical robots without simulators; that demonstrates the motivation for models that learn from real interaction, while the task-specific abstract does not establish broad transfer. [3] Meanwhile *Iso-Dream* highlights a structural issue: observed changes can include noncontrollable environmental dynamics, which should not be confused with action consequences when learning a model for decisions. [13] These foundations motivate the survey's three themes: model design for interaction, evaluation beyond appearance, and the limits of current evidence.

## Model design: latent dynamics, video generation, and action interfaces
A recurring design choice is where to represent the future. Latent-dynamics agents predict compact states and use them for control or planning; generative simulators instead emphasize rendering extended visual futures in response to actions. PAN describes an autoregressive latent-dynamics backbone paired with a video diffusion decoder, conditioning on history and natural-language actions; its paper also identifies temporal drift and accumulated rollout errors as challenges. [4] This architecture seeks both an internal predictive state and visually legible outputs, but the reported design does not by itself prove faithful long-horizon interaction.

For embodied settings, *EnerVerse-AC* injects predicted robot actions into video prediction and uses ray-map encoding for multi-view/camera motion, positioning generated environments as tools for policy data and evaluation. [5] The action interface is central: an attractive generated sequence is insufficient if changing an action does not cause a corresponding, repeatable change in the simulated future. AdaWorld (proceedings abstract) instead extracts latent actions from video in a self-supervised way and conditions an autoregressive model on them, aiming to reduce dependence on action-labelled data and support adaptation. [14] These approaches address different bottlenecks—rendering and camera-conditioned prediction versus action representation and supervision—so their claims should not be ranked without shared tasks and measurements.

## From prediction metrics to behavioral evaluation
Evaluation is shifting from next-frame or visual-quality measures toward interaction. *World-in-World* argues that visual quality does not guarantee task success and evaluates action-conditioned models in closed-loop embodied tasks; its paper reports that post-training on action-observation data and additional inference-time planning improve results in its benchmark setting. [6] This is an important methodological distinction: prediction fidelity measures resemblance, while closed-loop success tests whether an agent can use the model to achieve a goal.

WBench frames evaluation as multi-axis, covering visual quality, instruction/interaction adherence, consistency, and physical plausibility; its authors report 22 automatic sub-metrics validated against human judgments and find no model uniformly strong across dimensions. [8] The reported count and finding describe that paper's benchmark and tested models, not a universal ranking. WorldPrediction probes higher-level action-state understanding and procedural planning; the retrieved text reports frontier models at 57% on its world-modeling task and 38% on its procedural-planning task, versus humans solving both perfectly. [7] These numbers expose difficulty on that benchmark, but should not be generalized to all world-model tasks or model families. Together, these evaluations support reporting separate visual, causal/action, temporal, physical, and task-success outcomes rather than collapsing them into a single score.

## Grounding, controllability, and transfer remain distinct failure modes
Predictive systems can fail even when their frames appear coherent. *Iso-Dream* explicitly separates controllable from noncontrollable visual dynamics, underscoring that correlation between an action and an observed change is not necessarily causal control. [13] *World-in-World* similarly makes closed-loop task success a distinct criterion, while WBench's multidimensional framework treats interaction adherence and consistency separately from appearance. [6][8] In practice, these dimensions call for interventions: vary the action while holding the context fixed, test whether consequences persist across turns, and evaluate whether apparent physical regularities support task completion.

Transfer needs its own test rather than inference from training-environment reward or next-frame accuracy. WorldTest proposes reward-free interaction followed by behavior evaluation in a related challenge environment, explicitly targeting downstream use beyond next-frame prediction. [9] Its evaluation suite is bounded by the environments and tasks implemented in the paper, however, and cannot establish open-ended transfer. WorldPrediction's reported planning gap and World-in-World's benchmark-specific results likewise warn that abstractions and long-horizon behavior are not settled by short visual rollouts. [7][6]

## Trends and open problems
The retrieved work spanning 2024-10-09 to 2026-10-09 shows parallel expansion in physical-world learning, video simulators, and evaluation. HF daily lists dated 2024-10-24 and 2025-11-11 included *WorldSimBench* and *PhysWorld*, respectively; their entries describe simulator evaluation and robot learning from video generation, but these are HF AI-generated summaries rather than independent verification of full-paper findings. [10][11] A 2026 HF search result for *Worldscape-MoE* describes a mixture-of-experts model for heterogeneous action control; again, this is summary-level evidence only. [12] These records suggest active exploration, not a settled winning architecture.

Three open issues follow from the evidence. First, long-horizon consistency and causal controllability require repeated action-conditioned tests, not just visually plausible samples; PAN itself identifies drift/error accumulation, while interactive benchmarks make adherence and success explicit. [4][6][8] Second, benchmark breadth and comparability remain limited: the reported WorldPrediction numbers are task-specific, and WBench's own results vary by evaluation dimension. [7][8] Third, connecting generative models to robust real-world decisions remains an empirical challenge: embodied action conditioning and physical robot learning are promising directions, but summaries and paper-reported experiments cannot substitute for independent replications and held-out transfer evaluations. [5][10] Dates and evidence scope matter: this survey uses papers and dated list/search records actually retrieved in this run; the searches do not establish exhaustive coverage of the entire period through 2026-10-09.
